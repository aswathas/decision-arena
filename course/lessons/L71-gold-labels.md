# L71 - Gold labels: the scarcest resource

## Why this matters

Accuracy, Brier score and ECE - three of the four headline metrics in this
harness - cannot be computed at all without one thing: a record of what
*actually happened*. That record is the gold label. No labels, no accuracy.
And labels are the only part of this project that cannot be generated by
software, bought from a vendor, or re-run tomorrow. They are the scarcest
resource in the entire field, and everything you build on top of them
inherits their quality.

## The naive story

You have a battery of 50 cases. For each one you know what should happen -
you can look at the ticket and see it. So you write the gold labels yourself.
Done: an afternoon's work, 50 labels, and now you have a benchmark.

The naive story has a fatal flaw: **you are not the population the model
serves.** You are a technical person who reads tickets carefully and knows
your product. The humans who actually handled these tickets made different
calls under time pressure, with partial information, and sometimes they were
wrong. Your labels measure *your reading of the ticket*, not *what happened*.
When the model disagrees with you, you cannot tell whether the model is
bad or you are.

## What breaks

**Break 1 - a wrong gold label makes a good model look bad.** This is the
asymmetry that makes labelling so dangerous. Suppose the model routes a
borderline ticket correctly according to the team that actually handled it,
but your gold says "sales" and the truth was "billing". The model gets
marked wrong. Accuracy drops. And the damage is not just this one case: if
one class of your labels is systematically wrong, you have built a benchmark
that rewards the *wrong behaviour*, and the model that scores well on it is
the one that learned your mistake.

**Break 2 - humans disagree, and the disagreement is not noise.** Two
experienced humans label the same ticket differently 30% of the time on
borderline cases. That 30% is not sloppiness - it is the genuine ambiguity
of the task. If you collapse it by picking one person's answer, you have
picked a policy and called it a fact.

**Break 3 - labels drift silently.** You label 50 cases in March. In June a
product change means "billing" now includes "usage disputes", and half your
labels are out of date. Nothing errors. The numbers just quietly become
meaningless.

## The real thing

**An outcome label** is a record of what really happened, not of what
should have happened. The difference is the whole lesson. If a ticket was
routed to the billing team and billing fixed it in four hours, the outcome
label for "which team handled this" is `billing` - because billing handled
it. If no team handled it and it churned, the outcome label for "did this
get resolved" is `false`. Outcome labels are read out of a system that
already exists: the ticket queue, the CRM, the logs.

**Label quality** has two independent dimensions, and confusing them causes
most labelling disasters:

- **Validity** - is the label actually true? ("Was this really a billing
  ticket?")
- **Reliability** - would two people labelling the same thing produce the
  same label? ("Would both of them say billing?")

A label can be valid but unreliable (two experts disagree about a
borderline case - the task is genuinely ambiguous) or reliable but invalid
(everyone agrees, and they are all wrong - the taxonomy does not match the
work). Improving reliability on ambiguous cases usually means *improving
the task definition*, not adding more labels.

**Disagreement** between two labellers on the same case is data, not
noise. It tells you two things: how ambiguous this case is, and how much
your headline accuracy number is allowed to move. If two labellers agree on
95% of cases, your benchmark has a floor of roughly 5% inherent error. If
you report "the model scores 88%", you must also report that your labels
themselves are only 95% consistent - otherwise the number has a false
precision.

**Adjudication** is the step that resolves a disagreement, and it is a
*process*, not a vote:

1. Two labellers label independently. Neither sees the other's answer.
2. Where they differ, a third person - or a rule - resolves it.
3. The disagreement is *recorded*, because it marks the case as ambiguous
   and belongs in a separate "borderline" slice of the results.

The rule of thumb for adjudication: **the adjudicator's job is to pick the
label that the work actually justified**, not to pick the label most people
like. And the cases that got adjudicated should be kept and looked at
separately - they are the hardest cases in your set, and they are where a
model's competence is actually visible.

**What labels make computable.** Look at `metrics.collect_scored()`: for
every question with a gold entry, it produces a `(probability, outcome)`
pair. The probability comes from the model; the outcome comes from the
label. From those pairs alone:

- **Accuracy** - did the outcome (1 or 0) match the answer?
- **Brier** - how far was the probability from the outcome?
- **ECE** - when the model says 0.8, is it right 80% of the time?

No labels, no outcomes, no accuracy, no Brier, no ECE. And *that* is why
the format-friction experiment (L61) was so cheap: it needs neither.

**Bootstrapping labels from real work.** You do not have to hand-write
labels from scratch. The cheap path:

1. **Start from what the work already recorded.** Every ticket queue knows
   which team handled a ticket and how it was closed. That is a free
   outcome label for the routing question.
2. **Only label the cases the system is unsure about.** If a model agrees
   with the historical route on 90% of tickets, you do not need a label for
   those - you need labels for the 10% where it disagrees. Those are the
   cases worth paying a human for.
3. **Label the disagreement, not the whole set.** This is the trick that
   makes labelling affordable: the interesting signal is concentrated in the
   cases where the model and the history differ.
4. **Keep the borderline cases separate.** Do not average them into your
   headline number.

## In this repo

- `decisionarena/metrics.py` - `collect_scored(answers_by_qid, gold)` is
  where a gold label becomes an outcome. The docstring states the mapping:
  `bool` -> `noul` question, `str` -> `choice` question (the option key),
  `int`/`float` -> `score` question (the level index). A question with no
  gold entry is skipped entirely.
- `decisionarena/metrics.py` - `score_case()` returns `accuracy`, `brier`
  and `ece` from those pairs. All three are `None` when there are no gold
  entries, which is how the harness tells you it could not score the case.
- `decisionarena/metrics.py` - `_brier(probs, outcomes)` is
  `sum((p - o) ** 2) / len(probs)` - the mean squared distance between the
  stated probability and the realised outcome. `_ece(probs, outcomes)` puts
  the pairs into ten buckets and measures `|confidence - accuracy|`.
- `decisionarena/harness.py` - `run_battery()` only calls
  `metrics.score_case(...)` when `case.get("gold")` is truthy. No gold, no
  score - the call is still recorded in the JSONL, so the raw evidence
  survives even when the metrics do not.
- `decisionarena/harness.py` - `battery_hash(cases)` includes the gold
  labels in the fingerprint, because the gold is part of the case list. If
  you relabel a case, the hash changes - and that is exactly what you want
  to know, because your old numbers were measured against the old labels.
- `cases/routing.json` - five cases with `gold`:
  `{"is_billing": true, "team": "billing", "severity": 1}` for
  `routing-001`. Read them: `routing-005` (the spam ticket) has
  `"severity": 0` and `"team": "other"`.
- `cases/injection.json` and `cases/format_variants.json` - **no `gold`
  keys at all**. Those batteries are measured by answer change and by
  probability movement, so they need no outcomes. That contrast is the
  clearest illustration in the repo of what labels are for.
- `AGENTS.md` - hard rule 4: "The mock is not evidence." The mock scores
  badly against the real gold labels precisely because it is not a model.

keys.

It must contain:

- a function `adjudicate(label_a, label_b) -> dict` that takes two
  proposed labels for the same case and returns a dict with:
  - `agree` (a boolean)
  - `resolved` (the label you adjudicate to, and `None` when the two
    labels agree, because then there is nothing to resolve)
  - `reason` (a short string saying why, e.g. that the historical record
    says one thing, or that the case is ambiguous and belongs in the
    borderline slice)
- a function `label_quality(cases: list[dict]) -> dict` that takes the
  cases you loaded and returns:
  - `cases` (how many)
  - `with_gold` (how many have a non-empty `gold`)
  - `without_gold` (how many do not)
  - `questions_scored` (the total number of gold entries across all cases -
    this is the number of labelled outcomes you have, which is the number
    that determines whether accuracy is computable at all)
- a `main()` that:
  - loads the cases from `cases/routing.json` with
    `decisionarena.harness.load_cases`
  - loads the cases from `cases/injection.json` the same way
  - prints the `label_quality()` summary for both
  - demonstrates `adjudicate()` on two labels that **disagree** and on two
    that **agree**, and prints the result of each
  - asserts that `cases/routing.json` has a non-zero `questions_scored` and
    that `cases/injection.json` has zero, failing loudly otherwise

Inputs: the two case files, and two made-up pairs of labels. Expected
output: a summary showing the routing battery has labelled outcomes and the
injection battery has none, plus four adjudicated results.

**Hint:** the two batteries differ in exactly one structural field. Look at
what `run_battery()` requires before it will call `metrics.score_case()` -
